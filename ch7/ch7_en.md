### Third Chapter.

## The Production of Absolute Surplus-Value.

## 1\) *Labor Process and Valorization Process*

The use of labor-power is *labor itself*. The buyer of labor-power consumes it by making its seller *work*. In this way the latter becomes labor-power active *in actu*, *worker*, which previously he was only *in potentia*. In order to present his labor in *commodities*, he must above all present it in *use-values*, *things* that serve to meet needs of some kind. It is thus a particular use-value, a determinate *article*, that the capitalist has the worker make. The *production of use-values*, or *goods*, does not change its *universal* nature because it takes place *for* the capitalist and under his control. The *labor process* is therefore first to be considered in its *abstract* moments, independently of every *determinate social form*.

The labor process is first of all a process between the human being and nature, a process in which the human being mediates, regulates, and controls his metabolism with nature through his own action. He confronts the natural material itself as a force of nature. The forces of nature belonging to his corporeality—arms and legs, head and hand—he sets in motion in order to assimilate the natural material in a form usable for his own life. By acting through this movement upon the nature outside him and altering it, he at the same time alters his own nature. He develops the potentialities slumbering within it and subjects the play of its forces to his own command. We are not concerned here with the first animal-like, instinctive forms of the labor process. The condition in which the worker appears on the commodity market as seller of his own labor-power has been displaced into a primordial background from the condition in which the human labor process had not yet cast off its first instinctive form. We presuppose the labor process in a form that belongs *exclusively to the human being*. A spider performs operations resembling those of the weaver, and a bee puts many a human architect to shame through the construction of its wax cells. But what distinguishes the worst architect from the best bee from the outset is that he has built the cell in his head before building it in wax. At the end of the labor process there emerges a result that was already present at its beginning in the *worker's representation*, thus already *ideally*. It is not merely a change of form in the natural that he *effects, actualizes* in the natural; at the same time he actualizes *his purpose*, which he *knows*, which determines the manner of his activity as a law, and to which he must subordinate his will. And this subordination is not an isolated act. Besides the exertion of the organs that work, the *purpose-directed will*, which manifests itself as *attention*, is required for the whole duration of the labor process—and all the more so, the less the work, through its own content and its manner of execution, carries the worker along with it, and the less, therefore, he enjoys it as play of his own bodily and mental powers.

The simple moments of the labor process are *purposeful activity* or *labor itself*, its *object*, and its *means*.

*The earth* (under which, economically speaking, *water* too is included), as it originally equips the human being, without his intervention, with provisions, ready-made means of life,[^1] is found there as the *universal object* of human labor. All things that labor merely detaches from their immediate connection with the earth as a whole are objects of labor found ready-made by nature. Such are the fish caught after being separated from its life-element, water; the wood felled in the primeval forest; the ore broken from its vein. If, by contrast, the object of labor has already, so to speak, been filtered *through earlier labor*, we call it *raw material*. For example, ore already broken out, which is now washed. All raw material is an object of labor, but not every object of labor is raw material. An object of labor is raw material only once it has already undergone an alteration mediated by labor.

The *instrument of labor* is a thing or a complex of things that the worker inserts between himself and the object of labor and that serve him as *conductor* of his activity upon that object. He uses the mechanical, physical, and chemical properties of things in order to make them act as means of force upon other things, *in accordance with his purpose*.[^2] The object which the worker *immediately* takes possession of—leaving aside the taking of ready-made means of life, such as fruits, in which his own bodily organs alone serve as instruments of labor—is not the object of labor but the instrument of labor. The natural thing itself thus becomes an *organ* of his activity, an organ that he adds to his own bodily organs, extending his natural form, notwithstanding the Bible. Just as the earth is his original provision-chamber, so it is his original arsenal of instruments of labor. It supplies him, for example, with the *stone* with which he throws, grinds, presses, cuts, and so on. *The earth itself* is an instrument of labor, but its service as an instrument of labor in agriculture presupposes in turn a whole series of other instruments of labor and a relatively high development of labor-power.[^3] As soon as the labor process is developed to any degree, it already requires worked instruments of labor. In the oldest human caves we find stone tools and weapons. Alongside worked *stone* and *wood*, the *tamed* animal—that is, itself already altered through labor, bred—plays the principal role as an instrument of labor at the beginning of human history.[^4]

The use and creation of instruments of labor, although present in germ among certain animal species, characterize the *specifically human labor process*, and *Franklin* therefore defines the human being as “ *a toolmaking animal*,” an animal that fabricates tools. Relics of *instruments of labor* have the same importance for the knowledge of the organization of extinct animal species as bone relics have, and for judging extinct economic formations of society. It is not *what* is made, but *how* and with what instruments of labor it is made, that distinguishes economic epochs.[^5] Instruments of labor are not only the gauge of the development of human labor-power, but also the index of the social relations under which labor is performed. Among the instruments of labor themselves, the *mechanical instruments of labor*, whose totality may be called the *bone-and-muscle system of production*, offer much more decisive characteristic marks of an epoch of social production than those instruments of labor that serve only as containers for the object of labor, and whose totality can be designated quite generally as the *vascular system of production*, such as pipes, barrels, baskets, jugs, and so on. It is only in chemical manufacture that they play a significant role.

In the broader sense, the labor process counts among its *means*, in addition to the things that mediate the effect of labor upon its object and thus serve in one way or another as conductors of activity, all the *objective conditions* required for the process as a whole to proceed. They do not enter directly into the labor process, but without them the labor process cannot proceed at all or can proceed only imperfectly. The universal instrument of labor of this kind is once again *the earth itself*, for it gives the worker the *locus standi* and his process *the field of operation* (*field of employment*). Instruments of labor of this kind already mediated by labor include, for example, work buildings, canals, roads, and so on.

The *labor process* is thus a process in which human activity, through the instrument of labor, effects an alteration in the object of labor that was intended from the outset. This process expires in the *product*. Its product is a *use-value*, a natural material assimilated to human needs through a change of form. Through the process, labor has connected itself with its object. Labor is objectified and the object is worked upon. What appeared on the worker's side in the form of unrest now appears on the product's side as a quiescent property, in the form of being. He has spun, and the product is yarn.

If one considers the whole process from the standpoint of its result, *the product*, then both the *instrument of labor* and the *object of labor* appear as *means of production*[^6], and labor itself as *productive labor*.[^7]

When a use-value emerges from the labor process as a *product*, other use-values, themselves products of earlier labor processes, enter the process *as means of production*. The same use-value that is the product of one labor process forms the means of production of another. Products are therefore not only the result but at the same time the *condition* of the labor process.

With the exception of the *extractive industry*, which finds its object of labor ready-made by nature, as in mining, hunting, fishing, and so on (agriculture only insofar as it first breaks open virgin soil itself), all branches of industry treat an *object* that is *raw material*, that is, an object of labor already filtered through labor, itself already the product of an earlier labor process. Such, for example, is *seed* in agriculture. Animals and plants, which one is accustomed to regard as products of nature, are not only products perhaps of the labor of the previous year but, in their present forms, products of a transformation continued over many generations under human control and mediated by human labor. As for the instruments of labor proper, the great majority show traces of past labor to even the most superficial observer.

The raw material may form the principal substance of a product, or enter into its formation *only* as an *auxiliary material*. The auxiliary material is then consumed by the instrument of labor, as coal by the steam engine, oil by the wheel, hay by the draft horse; or it is added to the raw material in order to produce an alteration in it, as chlorine to unbleached linen, coal to iron, dye to wool; or it assists labor itself in its execution, as, for example, materials used for lighting and heating the workshop. The distinction between principal material and auxiliary material disappears in chemical manufacture proper, because none of the raw material reappears as a substance of the product.[^8]

Since every thing possesses various properties and is therefore capable of various uses, the same product can be the raw material of very different labor processes. Grain, for example, is raw material for the miller, the starch-maker, the distiller, and the cattle-breeder. It also enters as seed, raw material into its own production; coal likewise emerges as product from the mining industry and enters into it as a means of production.

The same product may serve in the same labor process both as instrument of labor and as raw material. In cattle-fattening, for example, where the cattle are the raw material, they at the same time serve as an instrument for producing manure.

A product may exist in a form ready for consumption and yet become once more the raw material of another product, as grapes do in wine-making. Or labor may release its product in a form in which it can be used only as raw material. Raw material in this condition, such as cotton, thread, and yarn, is called semi-manufactured, though it would be more accurate to call it material already manufactured to a certain degree. Although itself already a product, this raw material may have to pass through a whole series of different processes, in each of which it serves as raw material, constantly changing its form, until it is precipitated from the final process of the series as a finished product, either as a means of life or as an instrument of labor.

One sees: whether *a use-value* appears as *raw material, instrument of labor*, or *product* depends entirely on its *determination* in the labor process, on the position it occupies there; as its position changes, so does its determination.

Through their entry as *means of production* into new labor processes, products therefore lose their character as products. They function only as *objective factors* of living labor. The spinner treats spindles only as means for spinning, and flax as the material he spins. Of course, it is impossible to spin without material and spindles, and therefore the existence of these products is presupposed at the beginning of the spinning process. But in the process itself it is just as irrelevant that they are products of past labor as it is, in digestion, that bread is the product of the previous labor of the farmer, miller, and baker. On the contrary, it is through their defects that the means of production in any process remind us that they are products of past labor. A knife that will not cut, thread that keeps breaking, forcibly call to mind Mr. A, the cutler, or Mr. B, the spinner. In a successful product, the role of past labor in mediating its useful properties has been extinguished.

A machine that does not serve in the labor process is useless. Besides, it falls prey to the destructive force of natural metabolism. Iron rusts, wood rots, raw wool is eaten by moths. Living labor must seize these things, resurrect them from the dead, and transform them from merely possible into effective use-values. Licked by the fire of labor, transformed into its organs, called by its breath to fulfill their proper functions, they are also consumed, but for a determinate purpose, as formative elements of new products.

If existing products are not only the *results* but also the *conditions of existence* of the labor process, then, on the other hand, only by throwing them into it, by bringing them into contact with living labor, can these results of past labor be preserved and used.

In the labor process its material elements, its object and its instrument, are consumed *as use-values*. The labor consumes its material elements in order to produce material elements. The process of consumption of labor is at the same time the process of production of the commodity and of surplus-value. This productive consumption differs from individual consumption in that the latter consumes products as means of life of the living individual, while the former consumes them as means of operation of labor. The product of individual consumption is therefore the consumer himself; the result of productive consumption is a product distinct from the consumer.

Insofar as the instruments of labor and the object of labor themselves are already *products*, results of past labor processes, the labor process consumes products in order to create products, or uses products as means of production of new products. But the labor process which originally takes place between the human being and the earth, found outside him, never ceases to employ means of production of natural origin, which represent no combination of natural elements with human labor.

The *labor process*, as we have presented it in its simple and *abstract* moments, is purposeful activity for the production of use-values, the appropriation of the natural for human needs, the universal condition of the metabolism between human beings and nature, the eternal natural condition of human life, and therefore independent of every form of that life, or rather common to all its social forms. We had therefore no need to present the worker in relation to other workers. The human being and his labor on the one hand, nature and its materials on the other, were sufficient. The taste of the porridge does not tell us who grew the oats. An instrument of labor alone is enough to indicate the labor process in the past.

The worker now returns to our prospective capitalist. We left him after he had bought, on the commodity market, all the factors necessary to the labor process: its objective factors, the means of production, and its subjective factor, labor-power. With the keen eye of an expert, the capitalist has selected the means of production and labor-power appropriate to his particular business: spinning, the manufacture of boots, or whatever. Our capitalist then sets out to consume the commodity he has bought, labor-power—that is, he makes the bearer of labor-power, the worker, consume the means of production through his labor. The general character of the labor process does not, of course, change because the worker performs it for the capitalist instead of for himself. Nor does the particular manner in which boots are made or yarn spun change at first through the intervention of the capitalist. The capitalist must begin by taking labor-power as he finds it on the market, and hence also its labor as it arose in a period when there were as yet no capitalists. The transformation of the mode of production through the subordination of labor to capital can occur only later, and must therefore be considered later.

The labor process, as it proceeds as *the process of consumption of labor-power by the capitalist*, displays two characteristic phenomena.

The worker works *under the control of the capitalist*, to whom his labor belongs. The capitalist takes care that the work proceeds properly and that the means of production are used appropriately, so that raw material is not wasted and the instrument of labor is spared, that is, destroyed only insofar as its use in labor makes necessary.

Secondly, *the product is the property of the capitalist*, not that of the immediate producer, the worker. The capitalist pays, for example, the daily value of labor-power. Its use for the day, like that of a horse he has hired for a day, belongs to him. The use of the commodity belongs to its buyer, and the possessor of labor-power, by giving his labor, in fact gives only the use-value he has sold. From the moment he entered the workshop, therefore, the use-value of his labor-power, and hence its use, labor, belonged to the capitalist. By purchasing labor-power, the capitalist incorporated labor itself, as living ferment, into the dead elements forming the product, which also belong to him. From his point of view, the labor process is merely the consumption of the commodity labor-power that he has bought, but he can consume it only by adding means of production to the worker. The labor process is a process between things that the capitalist has bought, things that belong to him. The product of this process therefore belongs to him just as much as the product of the fermentation process in his wine cellar.

The product—the property of the capitalist—is a *use-value*, yarn, boots, and so on. But although boots are, for example, in some sense the basis of social progress, and our capitalist is a decided progressive, he does not make boots for their own sake. In commodity production, use-values are generally produced only because, and insofar as, they are the material substratum, the bearers of *exchange-value*. Our capitalist has two aims. First, he wants to produce a use-value that has exchange-value, an article destined for sale, a *commodity*. And secondly, he wants to produce a commodity whose value is greater than the sum of the values of the commodities required for its production, the means of production and the labor-power for which he advanced his good money on the commodity market. He wants not only to produce a use-value, but a *commodity*; not only use-value, but *value*; and not only value, but also *surplus-value*.

In fact, since we are concerned here with *commodity production*, we have so far evidently considered only *one side of the process*. Just as the commodity is itself a unity of use-value and value, its process of production must be a unity of the labor process and the process of value-formation.

Let us now consider the *production process* also as a *process of value-formation*.

We know that the value of every commodity is determined by the quantity of labor materialized in its use-value, by the socially necessary labor-time required for its production. This also applies to the product that our capitalist has obtained as the result of the labor process. We must, therefore, first calculate the labor objectified in this product.

Suppose the product is *yarn*.

For the production of yarn, its *raw material* was needed first of all—for example, 10 pounds of cotton. We do not need to investigate the value of the cotton, since the capitalist bought it on the market at its value, say 10 shillings. In the price of the cotton, the labor required for its production is already expressed as socially average labor. We will further suppose that the wear of the spindle, which we represent by all the other instruments of labor employed, amounts to 2 shillings. If a quantity of gold of 12 shillings is the product of 24 hours of labor, or two working days, it follows first of all that 12 shillings is objectified in the yarn.

The circumstance that the cotton has changed its form and that the consumed quantity of spindle has entirely disappeared must not mislead us. According to the general law of value, for example, 10 pounds of yarn are equivalent to 10 pounds of cotton plus one quarter of a spindle, if the value of 40 pounds of yarn = the value of 40 pounds of cotton plus the value of a whole spindle; that is, if the same labor-time is required to produce both sides of this equation. In this case the same labor-time appears once in the use-value yarn and once in the use-values cotton and spindle. Value is therefore indifferent to whether it appears in yarn, spindle, or cotton. The fact that the spindle and cotton, instead of lying side by side, are united in the yarn, just as the parts of the body are united in the human being, does not affect their value, any more than it would affect the value of the spindle if it had been broken into pieces and the value of the pieces were not diminished. Insofar as the labor-time required for the production of the cotton, the spindle, and the yarn is the same, the quantities of value represented by yarn and by cotton and spindle are the same. In the form of the yarn, the spindle and cotton are only buried, as it were.

The labor-time required for the production of the cotton is part of the labor-time required for the production of the yarn, whose raw material it is, and therefore is contained in the yarn. The same applies to the labor-time required for the production of the spindle, without whose wearing away or consumption the cotton cannot be spun.

Insofar, then, as the *value of the yarn*, the labor-time required for its production, is concerned, the *various particular labor processes* carried out at different times and in different places, which must be performed to produce the cotton and the spindle and finally the yarn, may be regarded as different successive phases of one and the same labor process. All the labor contained in the yarn is past labor. It is a matter of complete indifference that the operations required for its final production, that is, the spinning, were completed only recently, while the labor objectified in the cotton and the spindle was performed earlier, perhaps a year ago. If a definite quantity of labor, say 30 working days, is required to build a house, the fact that the last day of labor was performed 29 days after the first does not alter the total labor-time incorporated in the house. The labor-time contained in the material and instruments of labor can therefore be considered as though it had been spent in a previous stage of the spinning process, before the labor finally added in the form of actual spinning.

The values of the means of production expressed in the price of 12 shillings—the cotton and the spindle—are therefore constituent parts of the value of the yarn, that is, of the value of the product.

Only two conditions must be fulfilled. First, the cotton and spindle must actually have served in the production of a use-value. In our example, they must have become yarn.

Value is indifferent to which use-value bears it, but a use-value must bear it. Secondly, it is presupposed that only the socially necessary labor-time was spent under the given social conditions of production. If, for example, only 1 pound of cotton were needed to spin 1 pound of yarn, only 1 pound of cotton might be consumed in the production of 1 pound of yarn. The same applies to the spindle. Although the capitalist may have a fantasy of using golden spindles, the value of the yarn depends only on the socially necessary labor that is actually embodied in it.

We now know the *portion of value* that the means of production, cotton and spindle, form in the *value of the yarn*. It is equal to 12 shillings, or the materialization of two working days. We must now consider the part of the value of the yarn added by the labor of the spinner.

This labor must now be considered from a quite different point of view than during the *labor process*. There it was a question of purposeful activity for the transformation of cotton into yarn. The more purposeful the labor, the better the yarn, assuming all other circumstances remain the same. The spinner's labor was specifically different from other kinds of productive labor, and the difference was manifested subjectively and objectively in the particular purpose of spinning, its particular mode of operation, the particular nature of its means of production, the particular use-value of its product. Cotton and spindle serve as means of subsistence for spinning labor, yarn as its product. On the other hand, if the labor of the spinner is considered as value-creating labor, it is not distinguished from the labor of the cotton-planter and spindle-maker, or, to take a closer example, from the labor of the cotton-spinner, for whom cotton and spindle are means of production, and yarn the product.

During the labor process, labor continually passes from the form of unrest into that of being, from the form of movement into that of objectivity. At the end of one hour, the spinning movement is represented in a certain quantity of yarn, and therefore a certain quantity of labor, one hour of labor, is objectified in the cotton. We say labor, and the spinning of cotton, because only labor that is expended in a form suitable to its purpose counts as labor.

It is now decisively important that, during the duration of the process—that is, the transformation of cotton into yarn—only the socially necessary labor-time is consumed. Under normal conditions, if 10 pounds of cotton are transformed into 10 pounds of yarn by one hour of labor, 10 pounds of cotton embody two working days, and one hour of labor is added. The same labor-time is represented in 10 pounds of yarn as in 10 pounds of cotton plus the quantity of labor added by spinning. Therefore, in this case, 10 pounds of yarn embody two working days plus one hour of labor.

*Raw material* and *product* appear here in a quite different light than from the standpoint of the actual labor process. The raw material counts here only insofar as it absorbs a determinate quantity of labor. By this absorption it is in fact transformed into yarn, because the labor-power was expended and added in the form of spinning. But the product, the yarn, is now only a measure of the labor absorbed by the cotton. If 1 pound of yarn is spun in one hour, 10 pounds of yarn indicate 10 hours of spinning labor. Definite product-quantities, determined by experience, now represent nothing but definite quantities of labor, definite masses of objectified labor-time. They are only the materialization of one hour, two hours, one day of social labor.

It is quite immaterial here that the labor is spinning labor, its material cotton, and its product yarn, just as it is immaterial that the *object of labor* is already a product, a raw material. If the worker had not spun but made boots, the raw material leather would still have objectified in the boots the labor already contained in the leather. In this case, as in the former, the labor newly added would not be present in the specific form of bootmaking or spinning, but in the general form of human labor. Thus, in this consideration, the labor of spinning, bootmaking, or cotton-growing is simply *labor*, and nothing more.

When labor-power was sold, it was presupposed that its daily value was 3 shillings, and that six working hours were objectified in the latter. But this circumstance did not alter the past labor contained in the labor-power, nor the daily labor that it can perform. If half a working day is required to keep a worker alive for 24 hours, it does not follow that he cannot work a whole day. The value of labor-power and the value that its use creates in the labor process are therefore two different magnitudes. This difference in value was what the capitalist had in mind when he bought the labor-power. Its useful quality of being a source of value, and of more value than it itself has, is the particular service the capitalist expects from it. And in doing so he acts in accordance with the eternal laws of commodity exchange. In fact, the seller of labor-power, like the seller of any other commodity, realizes its exchange-value and alienates its use-value. He cannot receive the one without giving up the other. The use-value of labor-power, labor itself, belongs just as little to its seller as the use-value of oil after it has been sold belongs to the oil merchant. The possessor of money has paid the daily value of labor-power; therefore its use for the day, the day's labor, belongs to him. The circumstance that the daily maintenance of labor-power costs only half a day's labor, although labor-power can operate, work, for a whole day, and that the value it creates during a day is therefore twice its own daily value, is a particular piece of good fortune for the buyer, but in no way an injustice to the seller.

Let us now look at the *total value of the product*, the 10 pounds of yarn. In them, two and a half working days are objectified: two days contained in the cotton and the consumed spindle-material, and half a day of labor absorbed during the spinning process. The same labor-time is represented in a quantity of gold worth 15 shillings. The price corresponding to the value of the 10 pounds of yarn is therefore 15 shillings, and the price of one pound of yarn is 1 shilling 6 pence.

Our capitalist is taken aback. *The value of the product is equal to the value of the capital advanced*. The value advanced has not *valorized itself*, has produced no *surplus-value*; *money* has therefore *not* been transformed into *capital*. The price of the 10 pounds of yarn is 15 shillings, and 15 shillings were spent on the commodity market for the *constituent elements of the product*, or, which is the same, the *factors of the labor process*: 10 shillings for cotton, 2 shillings for the consumed spindle-material, and 3 shillings for labor-power. The *swollen value of the yarn* is no help, for *its* value is only the sum of the values previously *distributed* among cotton, spindle, and labor-power; and from *such a mere addition of existing values* no *surplus-value* can ever arise.[^13] These values are now all concentrated in *one* thing, but so they were in the sum of 15 shillings before it was split up through three purchases of commodities.

In and of itself, this result is not astonishing. The value of one pound of yarn is 1 shilling 6 pence, and for 10 pounds of yarn our capitalist would therefore have to pay 15 shillings on the commodity market. Whether he buys his private house ready-made on the market or has it built for himself, neither operation will increase the money spent on acquiring the house.

The capitalist who knows his way around vulgar economics may perhaps say that he advanced his money with the *intention* of *making more money* from it. But the road to hell is paved with good intentions, and he might just as well have intended to make money without producing.[^14] He threatens that he will not be caught again; in future he will buy the commodity ready-made on the market instead of manufacturing it himself. But if all his fellow capitalists do the same, where is he to find commodities on the market? And money cannot be eaten. He catechizes us: one should consider his *abstinence*. He could have squandered his 15 shillings. Instead, he has *productively consumed* them and made yarn out of them. But for that he now possesses yarn instead of pangs of conscience. He must certainly not fall back into the role of the *hoarder*, who showed us what comes of asceticism. Besides, where there is nothing, the emperor has lost his right. Whatever the merit of his *renunciation*, there is nothing here to pay extra for it, since the value of the product emerging from the process is only equal to the sum of the commodity-values thrown into it. Let him therefore console himself with the thought that virtue is its own reward. Instead, he becomes importunate. The yarn is useless to him. He produced it for sale. So let him sell it—or, more simply, in future produce only things for his own needs, a recipe already prescribed to him by his own house physician, *MacCulloch*, as a proven remedy against the epidemic of overproduction. He rears up defiantly. Was the worker supposed to create products of labor and produce commodities with his own limbs, in the blue air? Did he not provide him with the material *with* which and *in* which alone he could embody his labor? Since the greatest part of society consists of such have-nothings, did he not render society an immeasurable *service* through his means of production, his cotton and spindle—and not the worker himself too, whom, in addition, he supplied with means of life? And should he not charge for the *service*? But did the worker not render him the reciprocal service of transforming cotton and spindle into yarn? Besides, this is not a question of *services*.[^15] A service is nothing but the useful effect of a *use-value*, whether of a commodity or of labor.[^16] Here, however, what counts is *exchange-value*. He paid the worker the value of 3 shillings. The worker returned an exact *equivalent* in the value of 3 shillings added to the cotton: value for value. Our friend, a moment ago so swollen with capitalist self-importance, suddenly assumes the modest bearing of his own worker. Has he not himself worked? Has he not performed the labor of supervising, of overseeing the spinner? Does this labor of his not also create value? His own overlooker and manager shrug their shoulders. Meanwhile, he has already resumed his former expression with a cheerful smile. He was only teasing us with that whole litany. He does not care a farthing for it. He leaves these and similar paltry evasions and hollow pretenses to the professors of political economy, who are paid specifically for them. He himself is a practical man, who may not always consider what he says outside business, but always knows what he does in business.

Let us look more closely. The *daily value of labor-power* was 3 shillings because a *half working day* is objectified in it—that is, because the means of life needed daily to produce labor-power cost half a working day. But the past labor contained in labor-power and the living labor it can perform, its daily maintenance-cost and its daily expenditure, are two entirely different magnitudes. The former determines its exchange-value; the latter forms its use-value. The fact that a *half working day* is necessary to keep the worker alive for 24 hours in no way prevents him from *working a whole day*. The *value* of labor-power and its *valorization* in the labor process are therefore two different magnitudes. This *difference in value* was what the capitalist had in mind when he bought labor-power. Its useful property of making yarn or boots was only a *conditio sine qua non*, since labor must be expended in a useful form in order to create value. What decided the matter was the *specific use-value of this commodity*: to be a source of exchange-value and of more exchange-value than it itself has. This is the specific *service* the capitalist expects from it. And in doing so he acts in accordance with the eternal laws of commodity exchange. In fact, the seller of labor-power, like the seller of every other commodity, *realizes its exchange-value* and *alienates its use-value*. He cannot obtain one without giving up the other. The use-value of labor-power, *labor itself*, belongs no more to its seller than the use-value of sold oil belongs to the oil merchant. The possessor of money has paid the *daily value* of labor-power; its *use for the day*, the *day's labor*, therefore belongs to him. The circumstance that the daily maintenance of labor-power costs only half a working day, although labor-power can act, can work, for a whole day, and that the value created by its use during a day is therefore twice its own daily value, is a particular piece of good fortune for the buyer, but absolutely no injustice to the seller.[^15]

Our capitalist foresaw this case. The worker therefore finds in the workshop the means of production necessary not for a six-hour but for a twelve-hour labor process. If 10 pounds of cotton absorbed six hours of labor and were transformed into 10 pounds of yarn, 20 pounds of cotton will absorb twelve hours of labor and be transformed into 20 pounds of yarn. Let us consider the product of the extended labor process. In the 20 pounds of yarn, there are now objectified five working days: four in the consumed quantity of cotton and spindle, one absorbed by the cotton during the spinning process. But the sum of the labor represented in five working days is 30 shillings. This is therefore the price of 20 pounds of yarn. The pound of yarn still costs 1 shilling 6 pence. But the sum of the values of the commodities that entered the process—cotton, spindle, and labor-power—was 27 shillings. The value of the yarn is 30 shillings. The value of the product is greater than the value advanced for its production by 3 shillings. The money has been transformed into capital.

All the conditions of the problem are solved, and the laws of commodity exchange have in no way been violated. Equivalent was exchanged for equivalent. The capitalist, as buyer, paid for every commodity—cotton, spindle, labor-power—its value. He then did what every other buyer of commodities does: he consumed their use-value. The consumption of labor-power, which was also the process of production of the commodity, yielded a product of 20 pounds of yarn with a value of 30 shillings. The capitalist returns to the market and sells commodities, after having bought commodities. He pockets 3 shillings more than he originally threw into circulation. The whole course, the transformation of his money into capital, takes place within the sphere of circulation and at the same time does not take place within it. It takes place by means of circulation, because it is conditioned by the purchase of labor-power on the commodity market. It does not take place in circulation, because circulation merely prepares the process of valorization, which occurs in the sphere of production. “Tout est pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles.”

By transforming money into commodities that serve as material-formers of a new product or as factors of the labor process, by incorporating living labor-power into their dead objectivity, the capitalist at the same time transforms value, past, objectified, dead labor, into capital, value that swells itself, a monster animated as if by love, which begins to “work” as though it had the devil in its body.

If we compare the *process of value-formation* with the *valorization process*, the valorization process is nothing other than a process of value-formation prolonged beyond a certain point. If the process of value-formation lasts only up to the point at which the value paid by capital for labor-power is replaced by a new equivalent, it is a simple process of value-formation. If it continues beyond that point, it becomes a valorization process.

If we further compare the *process of value-formation* with the *labor process*, the latter consists in the *useful* labor that produces use-value. Here the movement is considered qualitatively, in its specific manner and purpose. The same labor process, considered as a process of value-formation, presents itself only quantitatively. Here it is a question only of the time that labor requires for its operation, or of the duration during which labor-power is usefully expended. Here, too, the commodities that enter the labor process no longer count as functional, materially determinate factors of purposeful labor-power; they count only as determinate quantities of objectified labor. Whether the labor is contained in the means of production or added to them through living labor, whether it is contained in cotton or in the spindle, or spun, makes no difference. What matters is that no more labor-time is required to produce the commodity than is socially necessary under the given conditions. If this condition is fulfilled, then the value of the product is determined by the quantity of labor materialized in it. The labor must be of socially average quality. If it is more complex than average labor, it counts only as multiplied simple labor, so that a smaller quantity of complex labor is equal to a larger quantity of simple labor. Experience determines this reduction. Whether a commodity is the product of one hour of complex labor or one hour of simple labor, its value is the same as that of any other commodity produced in one hour of labor of the same social average intensity.

But labor counts only insofar as the time consumed in producing the use-value is *socially necessary*. This includes various determinations. The labor-power must function under normal conditions. If the spinning instrument is the prevailing means of production in society, the worker must not be given a wheel instead of a spinning machine. Nor must he be given inferior cotton, such as cotton that breaks at every moment. In either case he would use more than the socially necessary labor-time to produce one pound of yarn, but this excess labor-time would not create value or money. The normal character of the objective factors of labor does not depend on the worker but on the capitalist. A further condition is the normal character of labor-power itself. In the branch of production where it is employed, it must possess the average skill, dexterity, and speed. But our capitalist bought labor-power of normal quality. This labor-power must be expended with the average degree of exertion, with the socially usual degree of intensity. The capitalist takes care of this, just as he takes care that his worker is not idle for a moment without necessity. He has bought labor-power for a definite period, and he insists on what is his. He does not want to be robbed. Finally—and for this purpose our capitalist has his own code of penal law—the raw material and instrument of labor must not be consumed wastefully, because wasted material or instruments represent superfluous quantities of objectified labor, and therefore do not enter into the product of value-formation.

We see: the distinction previously derived from the analysis of the *commodity* between labor insofar as it is a producer of use-value and the same labor insofar as it is a creator of value now appears as the distinction between the different aspects of the production process.

As *unity of the labor process* and the *process of value-formation*, the *production process* is the production process of commodities; as *unity of the labor process* and the *valorization process*, it is the capitalist production process, the capitalist form of commodity production.

It was remarked earlier that for the valorization process it is quite immaterial whether the labor appropriated by the capitalist is simple, unskilled, social-average labor, or more complex labor, labor of a higher specific weight. The labor which counts as higher, more complex labor in comparison with social-average labor is the expression of a labor-power into which higher costs of training enter, whose production costs more labor-time and which therefore has a higher value than simple labor-power. If the value of this labor-power is higher, it also expresses itself in higher labor, and therefore objectifies itself, in the same periods of time, in proportionally higher values.

However, whatever difference there may be between the labor of a spinner and that of a jeweler, the portion of the jeweler's labor by which he merely replaces the value of his own labor-power does not differ in any way, in its qualitative character, from the additional portion by which he creates surplus-value. Here surplus-value arises only through a quantitative excess of labor, through the longer duration of the same labor process: in one case the process of producing use-values, in the other the process of producing value.
